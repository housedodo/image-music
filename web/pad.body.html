Ten worlds

The picture first decides what kind of sound it becomes: Drift (the plain pad), Glass (bells placed by the picture's highlights), Tide (waves and a wind that finds the chord), Choir (formant voices, when there is a face), Pulse (an arpeggio, for repeating patterns and city lights), Titan (huge saw swells), Dust (tape wobble and crackle), Abyss (deep, with distant pings), Ember (fire and kalimba) and Aurora (slow high light). Colour, position, texture and, where an on-device image model can load, what is actually in the frame all vote. The chord then moves through two to four steps read from the picture's left-to-right strips, melodies use a scale chosen per picture, and a fingerprint of the pixels sets every small detail — so the same photo always sounds the same, and a different photo of the same thing still sounds a little different. Worlds you have found are remembered on this device.

What the picture controls

Fifteen measurements, no sequencer. Only two of them quantise — the root and the chord quality — so the odds of two unrelated photos giving the same patch are vanishingly small.

Hue → root
Circular mean of hue, weighted by saturation, stepped round the circle of fifths at 30° per step.
Feeling → chord quality
Two axes, not one. Valence (brightness, saturation, colour temperature) runs warm to cool; arousal (contrast, edge density, saturation, grain) runs still to busy. The pair picks one of nine chords from a grid: serene maj9, warm add9, joyful open add9, open sus2, suspended open add9, bright add9 (high 9th), wistful min9, moody min7, tense minor (dark, saw-heavy).
Saturation → voice count
Grey gives a bare open fifth; vivid stacks all six notes. Colour is harmonic content.
Vertical spread → voicing
Where the light sits in the frame decides close or open voicing across octaves.
Texture → detune
Local entropy drives each note's oscillators apart, 2¢ glassy to 18¢ choral.
Colour purity → focus
A band dominated by one hue plays fewer notes with tighter detune; a band of many colours adds a note and drifts wider. A genuine second colour in the frame adds its own chord tone up top, snapped into the key so it can never be the root.
Tonal range → layer count
Shadows, midtones and highlights each become a layer if they cover at least a tenth of the frame.
Brightness → filter cutoff
One lowpass: 300 Hz at night, 7 kHz at noon.
Contrast → resonance and swell
Peaky and moving, or flat and still.
Edge density → breath rate
All that's left of rhythm — one filter cycle per 40 seconds down to per 4.5.
Blur → reverb
Soft focus goes wet and distant; sharp stays dry and close.
Repetition → delay
Autocorrelation across a high-passed column signal finds any regular spacing — a fence, floor tiles, a row of windows — and its spacing becomes the echo time. Measured: a 16px fence gives 284 ms and a 32px window grid 478 ms, while ordinary photographs score 0.18–0.32 and get no echo at all.
Grain → chorus
Fine colour disagreement between neighbouring pixels sets the ensemble depth and rate.
Depth → pre-delay
A strong top-to-bottom luminance gradient reads as distance, so the reverb's pre-delay grows and the room gets bigger.

One photo, three layers

The layers come from inside the single picture. It is split into shadows, midtones and highlights, each measured separately: its own dominant hue, its own voice count, its own filter cutoff, its own position in the stereo field. Shadows play the bottom octave, highlights the top, and how much of the frame a band covers sets how loud its layer is. A band under 10% coverage is dropped — so a flat picture plays one layer and a picture with real tonal range plays three, decided by the photograph rather than by a setting.

Each band's hue is snapped into the key the whole picture set, so the layers can only ever add consonant tones. Nothing stacks across photos: every picture is its own pad, and taking a new one replaces the last.

Do everyday photos all sound the same?

They used to. Real photographs are not spread evenly across the measurement scales — they bunch up in the middle-low part of every one. Measured over twelve ordinary indoor and street scenes, saturation ran 0.02–0.37 and edge density 0.10–0.28, so almost the whole musical range was reserved for pictures nobody takes. Twelve such scenes produced only 6 distinct chords, across 3 roots, and G on eight of them.

Three changes fixed most of it, and the same twelve scenes now give 10 distinct chords across 5 roots:

Calibrated scales
Every feature passes through a logistic centred on the everyday median, stretching the crowded middle. It never quite saturates, so extreme pictures still separate at the ends.
Dominant hue, not average hue
The root now comes from the peak of a saturation-weighted hue histogram, so one red mug on a beige desk picks the key instead of being averaged into the desk.
Two axes for chord quality
Lightness used to drive quality, register and cutoff at once, which made them move together. Quality now reads lightness and warmth, so warm dark and cold dark part company.
Honest limit
Two photos of the same beige room should still sound alike — that is the mapping working. Check it yourself: save a few pads and the patch panel reports how far the current one sits from its nearest neighbour.

Using it in a track

Transpose before exporting — the stepper moves the root and the render follows, so you can match a pad to a song's key without re-shooting anything. The MIDI file holds the same chord as held notes, so you can re-voice it with your own instrument and keep the picture's harmony while dropping its texture. The loop is the opposite trade: it keeps the texture exactly, including the detune and the filter breath, which no MIDI file can carry. Most of the time you want both — the loop as a bed, the MIDI doubling it an octave down.
