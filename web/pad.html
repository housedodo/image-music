No grid, no tempo, no notes coming and going. Each image specifies a single sustained chord and the exact texture it's made of — and every image specifies a different one. Switch pictures while it's playing and the pad glides across rather than restarting.

Why a pad is the right reduction

Dropping rhythm removes the part of the old mapping that was doing the least honest work. A grid forced the image to pretend it was a sequence; a pad lets it be what it actually is — a static field with a colour, a density and a texture. Nine measurements, nine synth parameters, no sequencer.

Hue → root
Circular mean of hue, weighted by saturation, stepped round the circle of fifths at 30° per step. Two circles mapped onto each other exactly.
Lightness → chord quality & register
Bright images get major and lydian colour high up; dark ones get minor, phrygian ♭9, and drop an octave. This is the single biggest driver of mood.
Saturation → how many notes
Grey gives a bare open fifth. Vivid stacks a 7th, a 9th, a ♯11. Colour literally equals harmonic content.
Vertical spread → voicing
Where the luminance sits in the frame sets how far apart the notes are — a skyline voices wide across three octaves, a flat-lit close-up clusters inside one.
Texture → detune
Local entropy sets how far the three oscillators per note drift apart, from 2 cents (glassy) to 18 (choral and seasick). Rough surfaces sound rough.
Brightness → filter cutoff
One lowpass across the whole stack. Night shots sit at 300 Hz and sound muffled and near; noon sits at 7 kHz and sounds open.
Contrast → resonance & swell depth
High contrast gives a resonant peak and a deeper slow swell; flat images barely move.
Edge density → movement rate
The only thing left of rhythm: how fast the filter breathes, from one cycle per 40 seconds to one per 4. Busy images shimmer, calm ones drift.
Blur → reverb, colour spread → width
Soft focus goes wet and distant. Images whose left and right halves differ in colour spread wider in the stereo field.

Always a different pad — but always the same one for the same picture

Nine continuous measurements feed the patch and only two of them quantise (root and chord quality). The other seven are continuous, so the odds of two unrelated photos landing on the same patch are effectively nil — the fingerprint under the patch sheet is the raw vector, and you can watch it change as you switch images. What stays fixed is determinism: the same photo always yields the same pad, and a small change in angle or framing moves the continuous parameters slightly and the quantised ones not at all. A pad that drifts a little is still the same pad, which is exactly the tolerance a drone wants and a sequence doesn't.
